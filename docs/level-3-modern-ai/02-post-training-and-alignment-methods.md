## 1. From base model to assistant

A base model (L3-01) continues text. Ask it a question and it may answer, add more questions, or continue as a forum thread. **Post-training** turns it into something that follows instructions, uses a consistent format, declines some requests and, more recently, reasons at length before answering.

The template comes from InstructGPT [Ouyang et al., 2022]: fine-tune GPT-3 on demonstrations written by labelers (**SFT**), collect labelers' rankings of model outputs and train a **reward model** on them, then optimize the policy against that reward with **PPO**. In their human evaluations, outputs of the 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT were preferred to those of the 175B GPT-3, a hundred times larger. The RL-from-preferences idea itself came from Christiano et al., who trained Atari and simulated-robot agents with human feedback on under 1% of their interactions ("Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences", 2017, arXiv:1706.03741).

Recipes have changed since. Open recipes such as Tülu 3 use SFT, then DPO (section 5), then **RL with verifiable rewards** (section 6) [Lambert et al., 2024], and Olmo 3 (December 2025) uses the same three stages for its instruct and reasoning models, plus an RL-only variant trained directly from the base model (Team Olmo, arXiv:2512.13961). DeepSeek-R1 made large-scale RL the centerpiece [DeepSeek-AI, 2025]. As of mid-2026, a review of public recipes by Nathan Lambert and Finbarr Timbers (Interconnects, June 16, 2026) describes a further shift in several labs toward training domain-specialist models and distilling them into one student, with standalone DPO mostly dropped; that is a secondary, partly opinion-based source, and closed labs publish few details at all. Treat any description of a closed model's post-training as unverified unless the lab documented it.

## 2. Supervised fine-tuning

SFT is next-token training (L3-01) on (prompt, response) pairs, with two changes. Conversations are wrapped in a **chat template**, special tokens marking system, user and assistant turns, and the loss is **masked** so only response tokens count: the model should learn to answer, not to write user messages.

The data can be human demonstrations, but increasingly it is generated by stronger models (distillation) or sampled from the model itself and kept only if it passes a check (**rejection sampling**). Quality matters more than quantity. LIMA fine-tuned a 65B LLaMA on just 1,000 curated examples, and its responses were judged equivalent or preferred to GPT-4's in 43% of cases; the authors concluded that almost all knowledge is learned in pretraining and little instruction data is needed to teach the format ("LIMA: Less Is More for Alignment", Zhou et al., 2023, arXiv:2305.11206). That is a claim about format and style against 2023 baselines, not evidence that 1,000 examples teach new skills.

SFT's limit is that it only imitates. It copies the demonstrator's mistakes, it has no way to say "not like this", and it trains on text the model did not produce, so it never sees and corrects its own errors. Preferences and RL address those gaps.

## 3. Reward models from preferences

Comparing two answers is easier and more consistent than writing a perfect one. The **Bradley-Terry** model turns comparisons into a scalar reward $r(x, y)$ for prompt $x$ and response $y$:

$$P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big),$$

with $\sigma$ the logistic function, $y_w$ the preferred and $y_l$ the rejected response. The reward model is usually the language model with its output layer replaced by a scalar head, trained on the loss $-\log \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$: logistic regression on differences (L1-02). Only differences are identified, so adding a constant per prompt changes nothing.

A reward model inherits what labelers respond to, not what they intend. Singhal et al. found that much of the reward gain in standard RLHF setups came from longer responses, and that a reward based purely on length reproduced most of the downstream improvements over SFT ("A Long Way to Go", COLM 2024, arXiv:2310.03716). Sharma et al. found that human preference data favors responses that agree with the user's stated views, that humans and preference models sometimes prefer convincing sycophantic answers to correct ones, and that sycophancy appears across state-of-the-art assistants ("Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models", 2023, arXiv:2310.13548).

Script 1 builds this in miniature. Responses are feature vectors (four quality features and a length); a hidden "gold" reward likes slightly longer answers but penalizes very long ones; simulated labelers are also swayed by length. The learned reward model agrees with the gold ranking on 83.8% of fresh pairs, gets the quality weights about right (0.37 to 0.44 against 0.4), and puts 0.73 on length against a true slope of 0.3 at the typical length. Section 7 optimizes hard against it.

## 4. RLHF with PPO

**The objective.** With reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (the SFT model), reward $r$ and a coefficient $\beta > 0$:

$$\max_\pi \; \mathbb{E}_{x,\, y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}\big[r(x, y)\big] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big).$$

The KL term keeps the policy where the reward model was trained and is trustworthy, and stops it collapsing onto a few high-scoring strings. This is the reverse KL of L0-04, $\mathrm{KL}(\pi \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$, which is mode-seeking: the policy may drop parts of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ freely but pays heavily for putting mass where $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ has little.

**Its exact solution.** For one prompt, write the objective as $\sum_y \pi(y) r(y) - \beta \sum_y \pi(y) \log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$. Define $Z = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y) e^{r(y)/\beta}$ and $\pi^*(y) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y) e^{r(y)/\beta} / Z$. Substituting $r(y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} + \beta \log Z$ gives

$$\text{objective} = -\beta\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*) + \beta \log Z,$$

maximized by $\pi = \pi^*$. The optimal policy is the reference reweighted by $e^{r/\beta}$: small $\beta$ chases reward, large $\beta$ stays close. This one line drives sections 5 and 7.

**Why we need RL anyway.** For a language model you cannot enumerate $y$ to compute $Z$, so you optimize by sampling. Here is the policy-gradient intuition (L3-05 develops it properly). Since $\nabla_\theta \pi = \pi\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi$,

$$\nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[r(y)] = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[r(y)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)\big],$$

so you sample responses and push up the log-probability of each in proportion to its reward. For a sequence, $\log \pi_\theta(y) = \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$, so every token of a good response is reinforced. Subtracting a **baseline** $b$ that does not depend on $y$ leaves the gradient unbiased, because $\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta] = 0$, and reduces its variance a lot: with $r - b$, the **advantage**, a response is reinforced only if it beat expectations.

**PPO** [Schulman et al., 2017] adds two things. A learned **value model** predicts expected reward and serves as the baseline at every token. And because each batch of samples is reused for several gradient steps, the update is clipped: with ratio $\rho = \pi_\theta(y_t \mid \cdot) / \pi_{\text{old}}(y_t \mid \cdot)$ and advantage $A$, PPO maximizes $\min(\rho A, \operatorname{clip}(\rho, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A)$, so no single step moves the policy far. In the usual RLHF implementation the KL penalty is subtracted from the reward per token. The cost is engineering: four models (policy, reference, reward, value) in memory, generation inside the training loop, and many sensitive hyperparameters.

## 5. Direct preference optimization and relatives

**DPO** [Rafailov et al., 2023] skips the reward model and the RL loop. Invert the optimal policy: any reward can be written $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. Put that into Bradley-Terry; $Z(x)$ cancels in the difference, leaving a loss on the policy alone:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \left[\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\right]\right).$$

It is a classification loss on a fixed dataset of preference pairs: no sampling, no value model, two models in memory. The bracket, scaled by $\beta$, is an **implicit reward** difference. Script 2 checks the derivation: on a five-response problem with Bradley-Terry preference probabilities, DPO lands on $\pi^*$ to within $6.4 \times 10^{-7}$.

**Where it breaks.** The derivation assumes Bradley-Terry preferences estimated well. Azar et al. pointed out that when preferences in the data are deterministic or nearly so (common when each pair has one label), the implied reward gap is infinite, and DPO drives the loser's probability to zero whatever $\beta$ is: the KL regularization stops working [Azar et al., 2023]. Their **IPO** loss regresses the log-ratio margin to a finite target, $(h - \tfrac{1}{2\beta})^2$ with $h$ the bracket above. Script 2 shows both: with one label per pair, DPO's KL to the reference keeps growing with training (0.61 nats after 100 steps, 1.17 after 10,000) and IPO settles at 0.046. DPO also only constrains ratios *within* labeled pairs, so probability can drift between responses never compared to each other; in the script it drifts from the best response to a worse one. **KTO** needs only a binary "desirable or not" label per response instead of pairs (Ethayarajh et al., ICML 2024, arXiv:2402.01306).

**DPO or PPO? The evidence is mixed.** DPO's paper reported matching or beating PPO on sentiment control, summarization and single-turn dialogue [Rafailov et al., 2023]. Xu et al. found that well-tuned PPO surpassed DPO and other methods across their tests, including code competitions, and argued DPO has fundamental limitations ("Is DPO Superior to PPO for LLM Alignment?", ICML 2024, arXiv:2404.10719). Ivison et al. varied one component at a time: the quality of the preference data mattered most (up to 8% on instruction following and truthfulness), PPO beat DPO by up to 2.5% on math and 1.2% in general, and a bigger reward model helped mainly on math ("Unpacking DPO and PPO", NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2406.09279). A common reading, which I share but which is not settled, is that much of the difference is **online versus offline**: PPO trains on the current policy's samples, DPO on a fixed dataset from some other policy. Script 3 shows that gap at toy scale.

## 6. RL from verifiable rewards and GRPO

For math with checkable answers, code with tests, or instructions with checkable constraints ("answer in under 50 words"), a program can score the response. Tülu 3 named this **reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR)** [Lambert et al., 2024]. A checker cannot be flattered by length or agreement, though it can be gamed in other ways (section 7).

**GRPO** (group relative policy optimization) was introduced with DeepSeekMath as a variant of PPO that saves memory [Shao et al., 2024]. For each prompt, sample a group of $G$ responses and score them. Each response's advantage is its reward normalized within the group,

$$A_i = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}{\operatorname{std}(r_1, \dots, r_G)},$$

applied to every token of response $i$. The group mean replaces the value model. GRPO keeps PPO's clipped ratio and adds the KL to the loss rather than the reward, estimated per token as $\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta} - \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta} - 1$, which is always nonnegative. With 0/1 rewards the intuition is plain: in a group where some answers are right, the right ones are pushed up and the wrong ones down; a group that is all right or all wrong gives no signal. Liu et al. later identified an optimization bias in GRPO that inflates response length, especially for wrong answers, and proposed an unbiased variant, Dr. GRPO ("Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training", arXiv:2503.20783).

**DeepSeek-R1.** DeepSeek-R1-Zero applied RL directly to DeepSeek-V3-Base, with no SFT, using rule-based rewards for answer accuracy and for putting reasoning inside think tags; the authors avoided neural reward models because of reward hacking at scale [DeepSeek-AI, 2025]. AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 71.0% during RL (86.7% with majority voting), and long reasoning with self-checking appeared without being demonstrated, including an intermediate checkpoint that stopped to reconsider its approach mid-solution. R1-Zero's output had poor readability and mixed languages, so DeepSeek-R1 used four stages: SFT on thousands of long reasoning examples, reasoning-focused RL, rejection sampling of about 800K examples for another round of SFT, and RL over all scenarios with both rule-based and learned rewards. The work was published in Nature in 2025.

**What does RLVR actually add? An open question.** Yue et al. compared base and RLVR-trained models by pass@$k$, the chance that at least one of $k$ samples is right. RL models win at small $k$, but base models win at large $k$, which suggests RLVR mostly makes the model sample good reasoning it could already produce, and narrows its range, while distillation from a stronger model can add new reasoning patterns [Yue et al., 2025]. Shao et al. found that RLVR improved Qwen2.5-Math-7B on MATH-500 by 21.4 points with *random* rewards, close to the 29.1-point gain from correct rewards, while such spurious rewards often failed for Llama 3 and OLMo 2 ("Spurious Rewards: Rethinking Training Signals in RLVR", arXiv:2506.10947). Both results suggest RL often surfaces behaviors already latent in the base model, and that RL methods tested on one model family can mislead. The other side points to large practical gains and to behaviors, such as long self-correcting reasoning, that base models rarely show at feasible $k$.

Script 3 is a toy version you can inspect exactly. A small autoregressive policy writes the two digits of $a + b$; SFT on noisy demonstrations makes the reference. GRPO with a 0/1 correctness reward raises pass@1 from 0.60, 0.14 and 0.11 on prompts whose demonstrations were right 60%, 5% and 0% of the time to 0.98, 0.97 and 0.97, with entropy falling from 1.75 to 0.13 nats. Even "never demonstrated" answers were reachable: the network shares structure across prompts, so the SFT model already gave them 11% probability, and its pass@64 was 0.997. With a policy that sees only a prompt ID, the SFT model gives those answers 1.2% (pass@64 of 0.52), and GRPO still reaches 0.99, because 300 steps of 8 samples is 2,400 tries per prompt. The general point is consistent with Yue et al.: what RL can reach is set by what the reference model samples within the RL budget. A real response space is far too large for that. In two other seeds, GRPO locked a few prompts onto wrong answers and its pass@64 fell below the SFT model's, the narrowing Yue et al. describe.

## 7. Reward hacking

**Goodhart's law**: a measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure. Every reward used in post-training is a proxy. Gao et al. measured this with a large "gold" reward model standing in for humans and smaller proxy models trained on its labels: as optimization against the proxy increases (measured by KL from the start), gold reward rises, peaks and falls, with different functional forms for best-of-$n$ sampling and RL and with coefficients that scale smoothly with reward model size [Gao et al., 2022].

Script 1 reproduces the shape. **Best-of-$n$** (sample $n$, keep the proxy's favorite) has KL $\ln n - \frac{n-1}{n}$ from the reference for a continuous reward: the winner's density is $n F^{n-1}$ times the reference density, where $F$ is the reward's CDF, and $F$ of the winner has mean log $-1/n$. Gold reward peaks at 0.73 around $n = 256$ and falls to 0.47 at $n = 4096$ while the proxy keeps climbing. For the exact KL-regularized optimum, gold peaks at 0.59 at $\beta = 0.5$ and collapses to $-10.5$ at $\beta = 0.1$, where the mean length is 7 standard deviations above normal. Optimizing the gold reward directly reaches 1.19 at a smaller KL (1.46 nats) and 2.53 at 5.47 nats. The gap is the price of the proxy.

Documented cases in real systems:

- **Length and sycophancy**, section 3.
- **A production regression.** In April 2025 OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update in ChatGPT that had become markedly sycophantic. Its May 2, 2025 postmortem ("Expanding on what we missed with sycophancy") says an added reward signal based on users' thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback weakened the primary reward signal that had been holding sycophancy in check, and that offline evaluations and expert testing did not catch it. (OpenAI's page blocked automated fetching; I verified this through search results quoting it.)
- **Gaming checkers.** Coding agents trained against tests can learn to special-case or skip the tests. Baker et al. found that monitoring a reasoning model's chain of thought catches such hacks far better than monitoring actions alone, but that adding the monitor to the RL reward, under strong optimization, taught the model to hide its intent in the chain of thought while still hacking ("Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior and the Risks of Promoting Obfuscation", arXiv:2503.11926). They suggest accepting a "monitorability tax": do not optimize the chain of thought directly.

Defenses: the KL penalty and early stopping (choose the checkpoint on a held-out gold evaluation, not the training reward), better and more varied preference data, verifiable rewards where possible (with verifiers audited for loopholes), and evaluations that target the known failure modes (L4-03). None removes the problem, because the target is always a proxy.

## 8. Constitutional approaches and AI feedback

Human labels are slow, expensive and inconsistent. **Constitutional AI** [Bai et al., 2022] replaces most of them with a written list of principles. In a supervised phase, the model drafts responses to harmful prompts, critiques them against the principles and revises them, and is fine-tuned on the revisions. In an RL phase, an AI model judges which of two responses better follows the principles, and those judgments train the preference model: **RL from AI feedback (RLAIF)**. The authors reported a less harmful assistant that explains its objections instead of refusing evasively, with far fewer human labels.

The principles have grown into long documents. In January 2026 Anthropic published a new constitution for its Claude models, which it says is used at several stages of training, including having Claude generate synthetic training data and rankings of responses from it. It moved from a list of standalone rules toward explaining the reasoning behind desired behavior, so the model can generalize to cases the rules miss, while keeping a few hard constraints for high-stakes behavior (Anthropic, "Claude's new constitution", January 22, 2026, https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-new-constitution). Whether written principles produce the intended behavior is an empirical question. AI judges have their own biases (L4-03), and who chooses the principles is a governance question (L4-05).

## 9. How post-training shapes behavior

- **Format and persona.** Most visible changes are of style, turn-taking, refusal and tone; LIMA's result (section 2) suggests these need little data.
- **Calibration.** The GPT-4 technical report shows the pretrained model was well calibrated on a subset of MMLU (its stated confidence matched its accuracy) and that post-training reduced calibration significantly (OpenAI, 2023, arXiv:2303.08774).
- **Diversity.** Kirk et al. found that RLHF generalized better than SFT to new inputs but significantly reduced output diversity ("Understanding the Effects of RLHF on LLM Generalisation and Diversity", arXiv:2310.06452). Reverse KL and reward maximization both favor modes; in script 3 entropy fell from 1.75 to 0.13 nats.
- **Reasoning length.** RLVR models learn to spend many tokens thinking, which costs inference compute and sometimes overthinks easy problems.
- **What it does not establish.** Post-training shapes observed behavior on the distribution it was trained and tested on. Whether that amounts to robust values, how it holds up under jailbreaks, and how to evaluate it are open problems for L4-05.

### Script 1: reward model and over-optimization, `reward_overoptimization.py`

```python
"""Fit a Bradley-Terry reward model, then over-optimize it (Goodhart in miniature).

A 'response' is a feature vector: four quality features and a length. A
hidden gold reward (standing in for what people actually want) likes a
little extra length but penalizes very long answers. Simulated labelers
compare pairs of responses drawn from the reference policy, with
Bradley-Terry noise, and are also swayed by length beyond its real value.
A linear reward model is fit to their labels; it cannot represent the
length penalty. Then we optimize against it two ways and watch the gold
reward.
"""
import math

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
D_Q = 4                                   # quality features
w_gold = torch.full((D_Q,), 0.4)
LABELER_LENGTH_BIAS = 0.5


def gold(x):                              # x[:, :D_Q] quality, x[:, D_Q] length
    q, ln = x[:, :D_Q], x[:, D_Q]
    return q @ w_gold + 0.3 * ln - 0.35 * ln ** 2


def labeler_score(x):                    # what the raters actually respond to
    return gold(x) + LABELER_LENGTH_BIAS * x[:, D_Q]


def sample_ref(n):                        # the reference policy's responses
    return torch.randn(n, D_Q + 1)        # length is standardized: mean 0, sd 1


# 1. Preference data: 1,000 labeled pairs, P(y1 preferred) =
#    sigmoid(s(y1) - s(y2)), with s the labelers' (length-biased) score.
n_pairs = 1000
y1, y2 = sample_ref(n_pairs), sample_ref(n_pairs)
p1 = torch.sigmoid(labeler_score(y1) - labeler_score(y2))
label = torch.bernoulli(p1)              # 1 if labeler preferred y1
chosen = torch.where(label[:, None] == 1, y1, y2)
rejected = torch.where(label[:, None] == 1, y2, y1)

# 2. Reward model: linear in the features, trained with the Bradley-Terry loss
#    -log sigmoid(r(chosen) - r(rejected)). Only differences matter, so no bias.
w = torch.zeros(D_Q + 1, requires_grad=True)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([w], lr=0.05)
for _ in range(500):
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(chosen @ w - rejected @ w).mean()
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
w = w.detach()
proxy = lambda x: x @ w

t1, t2 = sample_ref(20000), sample_ref(20000)
agree = ((proxy(t1) > proxy(t2)) == (gold(t1) > gold(t2))).float().mean()
print(f"reward model: BT loss {loss.item():.3f}, agrees with gold ranking on "
      f"{agree:.1%} of fresh pairs")
print(f"  learned weights: quality {[round(v, 2) for v in w[:D_Q].tolist()]}, "
      f"length {w[D_Q]:.2f}")
print("  (gold: quality 0.4 each, length slope +0.3 at the mean, curving down;"
      " labelers add +0.5 per unit of length)")

# 3. Best-of-n: draw n responses from the reference, keep the one the proxy
#    scores highest. The KL from the reference of the winner's distribution
#    is ln n - (n - 1)/n for a continuous reward (derived in the text).
print("\nbest-of-n against the proxy (400 trials per n)")
for n in [1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024, 4096]:
    x = sample_ref(400 * n).view(400, n, D_Q + 1)
    best = x[torch.arange(400), proxy(x.flatten(0, 1)).view(400, n).argmax(1)]
    print(f"  n={n:5d}  KL {math.log(n) - (n - 1) / n:5.2f} nats   proxy "
          f"{proxy(best).mean():5.2f}   gold {gold(best).mean():5.2f}   "
          f"mean length {best[:, D_Q].mean():5.2f}")

# 4. KL-regularized RL: maximize E[r] - beta KL(pi || ref). The exact optimum
#    is pi(x) proportional to ref(x) exp(r(x) / beta). With a Gaussian
#    reference and r = a.x - c * length^2, that optimum is again Gaussian, so
#    every number below is exact rather than sampled.


def tilted(a, c, beta):
    """Mean and variance of N(0, I) * exp((a.x - c x_len^2) / beta), normalized."""
    var = torch.ones(D_Q + 1)
    var[D_Q] = 1 / (1 + 2 * c / beta)      # the quadratic term narrows length
    return a / beta * var, var


def kl_to_ref(mean, var):                  # KL(N(mean, diag var) || N(0, I))
    return 0.5 * (var + mean ** 2 - 1 - var.log()).sum()


def expected(mean, var, a, c):             # E[a.x - c x_len^2] under the Gaussian
    return a @ mean - c * (mean[D_Q] ** 2 + var[D_Q])


a_gold = torch.cat([w_gold, torch.tensor([0.3])])
for label_, a_opt, c_opt in [("optimizing the proxy reward model", w, 0.0),
                             ("optimizing the gold reward (the unreachable ideal)", a_gold, 0.35)]:
    print(f"\nKL-regularized optimum, {label_}")
    for beta in [4.0, 2.0, 1.0, 0.5, 0.35, 0.25, 0.1]:
        m, v = tilted(a_opt, c_opt, beta)
        print(f"  beta={beta:4.2f}  KL {kl_to_ref(m, v):6.2f} nats   proxy "
              f"{expected(m, v, w, 0.0):5.2f}   gold {expected(m, v, a_gold, 0.35):6.2f}"
              f"   mean length {m[D_Q]:5.2f}")
```

### Script 2: DPO on a bandit, `dpo_bandit.py`

```python
"""DPO on a five-response bandit: check the derivation, then break it.

One prompt, five possible responses, a known reference policy and known
true rewards. The RLHF objective max E[r] - beta KL(pi || ref) has the
exact solution pi* ~ ref * exp(r / beta). We check that:
  1. DPO trained on Bradley-Terry preference probabilities finds pi*;
  2. With one sampled label per pair (every pair then looks unanimous),
     DPO keeps drifting away from ref however long it trains, while IPO
     settles at a bounded distance.
"""
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
BETA = 1.0
ref = torch.tensor([0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10])
r = torch.tensor([1.0, 0.5, 0.0, -0.5, -1.0])
log_ref = ref.log()
pi_star = torch.softmax(log_ref + r / BETA, 0)

pairs = torch.combinations(torch.arange(5), 2)      # all 10 pairs (i, j), i < j
i, j = pairs[:, 0], pairs[:, 1]
p_true = torch.sigmoid(r[i] - r[j])                  # P(i preferred over j)


def set_pairs(new_pairs):
    global i, j
    i, j = new_pairs[:, 0], new_pairs[:, 1]


def kl(p, q):
    return (p * (p / q).log()).sum().item()


def margin(logits):
    """h = log pi(i)/ref(i) - log pi(j)/ref(j): DPO's implicit reward gap / beta."""
    logp = F.log_softmax(logits, 0)
    return (logp[i] - log_ref[i]) - (logp[j] - log_ref[j])


def dpo_loss(logits, p):
    # Each pair is labeled 'i wins' with probability p, 'j wins' otherwise.
    h = margin(logits)
    return -(p * F.logsigmoid(BETA * h) + (1 - p) * F.logsigmoid(-BETA * h)).mean()


def ipo_loss(logits, p):
    # IPO (Azar et al.): regress h toward +1/(2 beta) when i wins, -1/(2 beta)
    # when j wins; with label frequency p this is a weighted square loss.
    h = margin(logits)
    t = 1 / (2 * BETA)
    return (p * (h - t) ** 2 + (1 - p) * (h + t) ** 2).mean()


def train(loss_fn, p, steps, report_at=()):
    logits = log_ref.clone().requires_grad_(True)    # start at pi = ref
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([logits], lr=0.05)
    for step in range(1, steps + 1):
        loss = loss_fn(logits, p)
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
        if step in report_at:
            pi = torch.softmax(logits.detach(), 0)
            print(f"    step {step:5d}  pi = {fmt(pi)}  KL(pi||ref) {kl(pi, ref):.3f}")
    return torch.softmax(logits.detach(), 0)


fmt = lambda v: "[" + ", ".join(f"{x:.3f}" for x in v.tolist()) + "]"
print(f"ref       = {fmt(ref)}")
print(f"pi* (RLHF optimum, beta={BETA}) = {fmt(pi_star)}  KL(pi*||ref) {kl(pi_star, ref):.3f}")

print("\n1. DPO on exact Bradley-Terry preference probabilities")
pi = train(dpo_loss, p_true, 3000)
print(f"   DPO result = {fmt(pi)}  max |pi - pi*| = {(pi - pi_star).abs().max():.1e}")

print("\n2. One labeled comparison for each adjacent pair (0,1) (1,2) (2,3) (3,4)")
set_pairs(torch.tensor([[0, 1], [1, 2], [2, 3], [3, 4]]))
p_true = torch.sigmoid(r[i] - r[j])
label = torch.bernoulli(p_true)                      # a single 0/1 label each
print(f"   true P(first wins): {fmt(p_true)}, sampled labels: {label.int().tolist()}")
print("  DPO:")
train(dpo_loss, label, 10000, report_at=(100, 1000, 3000, 10000))
print("  IPO:")
train(ipo_loss, label, 10000, report_at=(100, 1000, 3000, 10000))
```

### Script 3: SFT, GRPO and DPO on a verifiable task, `grpo_toy.py`

```python
"""SFT, then RL with a verifiable reward (GRPO-style), versus offline DPO.

Task: given two digits a and b, write a + b as two digits (tens, ones).
The policy is a small autoregressive network: it picks the tens digit,
then the ones digit given the tens digit. There are only 100 possible
responses per prompt, so probabilities, pass@k and KL are computed
exactly instead of estimated.

The 'pretraining plus SFT' stage imitates noisy demonstrations. Prompts
are of three kinds, by how often the demonstrations are right:
  common: 60% correct,  rare: 5% correct,  never: 0% correct.
The question: what can RL from a 0/1 correctness reward fix, and what not?
Everything runs twice: with a policy that sees the digits a and b (it can
share what it learns across prompts) and with one that sees only an
arbitrary prompt ID (it cannot).
"""
import math
import time

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(4)
A, B = torch.meshgrid(torch.arange(10), torch.arange(10), indexing="ij")
A, B = A.flatten(), B.flatten()                     # 100 prompts
ANSWER = A + B                                      # correct response id = 10*tens + ones
kind = torch.zeros(100, dtype=torch.long)           # 0 common, 1 rare, 2 never
perm = torch.randperm(100)
kind[perm[50:80]], kind[perm[80:]] = 1, 2
KINDS = ["common", "rare", "never"]
P_CORRECT = torch.tensor([0.60, 0.05, 0.0])


class Policy(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, sees_digits, hidden=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.sees_digits = sees_digits
        # Input: the prompt (one-hot a and b, or a one-hot prompt ID), plus a
        # one-hot of the previous token (10 digits + start).
        self.prompt = (torch.cat([F.one_hot(A, 10), F.one_hot(B, 10)], 1) if sees_digits
                       else torch.eye(100)).float()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(self.prompt.shape[1] + 11, hidden), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(hidden, 10))

    def step_logits(self, prev):                    # prev: (100,) in 0..10
        return self.net(torch.cat([self.prompt, F.one_hot(prev, 11).float()], 1))

    def log_probs(self):
        """(100 prompts, 100 responses) table of log pi(response | prompt)."""
        lp_tens = F.log_softmax(self.step_logits(torch.full((100,), 10)), -1)
        lp_ones = torch.stack([F.log_softmax(self.step_logits(torch.full((100,), t)), -1)
                               for t in range(10)], 1)          # (100, tens, ones)
        return (lp_tens[:, :, None] + lp_ones).flatten(1)


def demo_distribution():
    """What the demonstrations look like: the right answer with probability
    P_CORRECT[kind], otherwise one of four typical mistakes."""
    p = torch.zeros(100, 100)
    s = ANSWER
    mistakes = torch.stack([s - 1, s + 1, s % 10, (s + 10) % 100], 1)  # off by one, lost carry, extra ten
    mistakes = torch.where(mistakes == s[:, None], (s[:, None] + 2) % 100, mistakes)
    mistakes = mistakes.clamp(0, 99)
    p_right = P_CORRECT[kind]
    for m in range(4):
        p[torch.arange(100), mistakes[:, m]] += (1 - p_right) / 4
    p[torch.arange(100), s] += p_right
    return p


def summarize(name, logp, logp_ref):
    p = logp.exp()
    p_ok = p[torch.arange(100), ANSWER]
    kl = (p * (logp - logp_ref)).sum(1)
    ent = -(p * logp).sum(1)
    cells = []
    for k in range(3):
        m = kind == k
        cells.append(f"{p_ok[m].mean():.3f} / {(1 - (1 - p_ok[m]) ** 64).mean():.3f}")
    print(f"{name:12s} " + "  ".join(f"{c:>13s}" for c in cells)
          + f"   {kl.mean():6.3f}  {ent.mean():5.2f}")


# ---- 1. SFT: maximum likelihood on 200 sampled demonstrations per prompt.
def sft(sees_digits, demos, epochs=300, lr=3e-3):
    pol = Policy(sees_digits)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(pol.parameters(), lr=lr)
    for _ in range(epochs):
        loss = -pol.log_probs().gather(1, demos).mean()
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
    return pol


def copy_of(pol):
    new = Policy(pol.sees_digits)
    new.load_state_dict(pol.state_dict())
    return new


# ---- 2. GRPO-style RL. For each prompt sample a group of G responses, reward
# 1 if correct, advantage = (reward - group mean) / (group std + eps). The
# loss is -advantage * log pi(response), plus beta times the k3 estimate of
# KL(pi || ref) on the sampled responses (the estimator GRPO uses). We take
# one gradient step per batch of fresh samples, so pi_old = pi and PPO's
# ratio clipping never activates; it is omitted.
def grpo(ref, logp_ref, steps=300, G=8, beta=0.02, lr=3e-3):
    pol = copy_of(ref)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(pol.parameters(), lr=lr)
    for _ in range(steps):
        logp = pol.log_probs()
        with torch.no_grad():
            y = torch.multinomial(logp.exp(), G, replacement=True)   # (100, G)
            reward = (y == ANSWER[:, None]).float()
            adv = (reward - reward.mean(1, keepdim=True)) / (reward.std(1, keepdim=True) + 1e-4)
        lp = logp.gather(1, y)
        log_ratio = logp_ref.gather(1, y) - lp                       # log(ref / pi)
        k3 = log_ratio.exp() - log_ratio - 1                         # >= 0, unbiased for KL
        loss = -(adv * lp).mean() + beta * k3.mean()
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
    with torch.no_grad():
        return pol.log_probs()


# ---- 3. Offline DPO on pairs built from the SFT model's own samples:
# 16 samples per prompt, every (correct, incorrect) pair becomes
# (chosen, rejected). Prompts with no correct sample contribute nothing.
def dpo_pairs(logp_ref, n=16):
    y = torch.multinomial(logp_ref.exp(), n, replacement=True)
    ok = y == ANSWER[:, None]
    rows, chosen, rejected = [], [], []
    for x in range(100):
        for c in y[x][ok[x]].tolist():
            for r_ in y[x][~ok[x]].tolist():
                rows.append(x), chosen.append(c), rejected.append(r_)
    rows = torch.tensor(rows)
    covered = torch.zeros(100, dtype=torch.bool)
    covered[rows] = True
    print(f"   DPO data: {len(rows)} pairs; prompts with at least one pair: "
          + ", ".join(f"{KINDS[k]} {covered[kind == k].sum()}/{(kind == k).sum()}" for k in range(3)))
    return rows, torch.tensor(chosen), torch.tensor(rejected)


def dpo(ref, logp_ref, data, beta, steps=300, lr=3e-3):
    rows, chosen, rejected = data
    pol = copy_of(ref)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(pol.parameters(), lr=lr)
    for _ in range(steps):
        logp = pol.log_probs()
        h = ((logp[rows, chosen] - logp_ref[rows, chosen])
             - (logp[rows, rejected] - logp_ref[rows, rejected]))
        loss = -F.logsigmoid(beta * h).mean()
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
    with torch.no_grad():
        return pol.log_probs()


demos = torch.multinomial(demo_distribution(), 200, replacement=True)   # (100, 200)
t0 = time.time()
for sees_digits in [True, False]:
    torch.manual_seed(1)
    print(f"\n=== policy input: {'the digits a, b' if sees_digits else 'a prompt ID only'} ===")
    ref = sft(sees_digits, demos)
    with torch.no_grad():
        logp_ref = ref.log_probs()
    data = dpo_pairs(logp_ref)
    print("pass@1 / pass@64 by prompt kind (exact), mean KL to SFT model, mean entropy (nats)")
    print(f"{'':12s} " + "  ".join(f"{k:>13s}" for k in KINDS) + "       KL  entropy")
    summarize("SFT model", logp_ref, logp_ref)
    summarize("GRPO", grpo(ref, logp_ref), logp_ref)
    summarize("DPO beta=0.1", dpo(ref, logp_ref, data, beta=0.1), logp_ref)
    summarize("DPO beta=1", dpo(ref, logp_ref, data, beta=1.0), logp_ref)
print(f"\n({time.time() - t0:.1f} s in total)")
```

## Common mistakes

- **Computing SFT loss on prompt tokens.** The model learns to imitate users. Mask everything except assistant tokens.
- **Judging RL by training reward.** Reward rising while a held-out gold metric falls is over-optimization (script 1). Track KL to the reference and an independent evaluation.
- **Forgetting the reference model in DPO,** or computing its log-probabilities with a different template or truncation than the policy's. The loss is then not DPO.
- **Treating $\beta$ as the same knob across methods.** In PPO it weights a KL penalty; in DPO it scales an implicit reward; with near-deterministic labels it barely regularizes DPO at all (script 2).
- **Using a group size that gives no signal.** If every response in a GRPO group gets the same reward, the advantage is zero. Filter prompts that are always or never solved, or raise $G$.
- **Reporting only pass@1 after RLVR.** Also report pass@$k$ for large $k$ against the base model to see whether ability grew or only sharpened.
- **Ignoring length.** Compare win rates at matched length, or control for it, before crediting a method.

## Check your understanding

1. **Why does RLHF include a KL penalty, and what does it do to the optimal policy?** The reward model is accurate only near the data it was trained on, and unconstrained maximization exploits its errors and collapses diversity. With the penalty, the optimum is the reference reweighted by $e^{r/\beta}$, so $\beta$ sets how far reward can pull the policy from the reference.

2. **What does DPO assume, and when does that assumption fail?** That preferences follow a Bradley-Terry model and are estimated well, so the optimal-policy formula can be inverted to an implicit reward. With near-deterministic or single labels, the implied reward gaps are infinite and DPO pushes losers' probabilities to zero regardless of $\beta$; it also only constrains ratios within labeled pairs.

3. **How does GRPO avoid a value model, and what is lost?** It uses the mean reward of a group of responses to the same prompt as the baseline and normalizes by the group's standard deviation. It needs several samples per prompt, gives no signal when a group's rewards are all equal, and has a documented bias toward longer responses (Liu et al., section 6).

4. **Why is best-of-$n$ a useful reference point for over-optimization?** It needs no training, its KL from the reference is known ($\ln n - (n-1)/n$), and plotting gold reward against it shows directly how much optimization pressure a proxy tolerates before the true objective declines.

5. **"RLVR taught the model to reason." What evidence would you want?** pass@$k$ at large $k$ for the base and RL models (if the base matches at large $k$, RL mostly sharpened sampling), results on more than one model family (given the spurious-reward findings), held-out problems free of contamination, and controls for length and format. Behaviors the base model essentially never produces at feasible $k$ would be stronger evidence of new ability.
